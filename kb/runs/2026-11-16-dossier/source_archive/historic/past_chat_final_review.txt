I think your criticism is justified. We've repeatedly identified a new mechanism, proved something about a restricted family, and described that mechanism as the next major direction. That has produced useful mathematics, but it hasn't yet established which approach has the greatest chance of yielding a genuinely significant result.
The broader literature changes the picture in two important ways.
First, the high-average-degree version of Erdős–Gyárfás has already been solved. Liu and Montgomery proved that every graph with sufficiently large constant average degree contains a power-of-two cycle. Their stronger theorem produces every even cycle length throughout a substantial interval.

2. The most important literature we should add to our working bibliography
A. Cycles of many lengths in Hamiltonian graphs
Bucić, Gishboliner & Sudakov · Forum of Mathematics, Sigma · 2022
Read the open-access paper
This is the paper most directly relevant to our recent chord research.
The authors already exploit parallel and interlacing chords, reroute Hamiltonian paths, and combine path-length sets at different scales. Their proof constructs many lengths by putting tightly clustered lengths together with widely separated ones.
The conceptual resemblance to our Passes XXV–XXVI is substantial. In fact, their Section 2 develops a more sophisticated version of the multiscale viewpoint we've been discussing.
Crucially, they identify controlling the distribution of lengths as the next difficulty. Their Section 5 proposes further questions about concentrated and widely spaced path-length families. 

Cambridge Core
+1
Implication: We should work forward from this paper's machinery. Building an independent chord-intersection framework from scratch risks reproducing a weaker version of existing mathematics.
B. A solution to Erdős and Hajnal's odd cycle problem
Liu & Montgomery · Journal of the American Mathematical Society · 2023
Read the research paper
This is arguably the deepest positive result near the original conjecture.
The authors obtain a long interval of consecutive even cycle lengths in sufficiently high-average-degree graphs. The underlying machinery uses expansion, paths of approximately prescribed length, and gadgets that repair the remaining length discrepancy.
It demonstrates that approximate length followed by exact correction is a viable proof architecture in graph theory.
The barrier is its degree hypothesis. Cubic graphs have average degree exactly three, whereas the theorem requires a sufficiently large constant. 

sciencedirect.com
+1
Implication: Rather than proving that sparse graphs have many lengths, investigate whether one can reproduce the approximate-length-plus-correction mechanism with only degree three, perhaps under a suitable connectivity condition.
C. Cycle lengths in expanding graphs
Friedman & Krivelevich · 2019–2021
Read the paper
This paper makes the distinction between approximate and exact cycle lengths particularly clear.
Ordinary constant expansion guarantees cycles within a bounded additive error of target lengths throughout a substantial range. A stronger expansion condition, requiring edges between any two sufficiently large disjoint vertex sets, forces an actual interval of cycle lengths. 

arxiv.org
The first result approaches our problem but cannot guarantee an exact dyadic length. The second already implies dyadic cycles in sufficiently large graphs satisfying that stronger condition.
Implication: The next meaningful question is whether cubic graph structure provides the exact-length correction that ordinary expansion lacks.

The odd-square parity result is worth retaining. It is a coherent mathematical statement, and its proof is short. I have not established whether it has already appeared elsewhere in equivalent form.
However, high-girth cubic graphs contain no squares at all. Our square-incidence method is consequently silent on a substantial class of difficult graphs.
The same issue affects several other directions: we often obtain strong conclusions by starting from configurations that a hypothetical counterexample may simply avoid.

Option 1: Scale-aware path-length control
This is my preferred direction.
The shared theme among Liu–Montgomery, Bucić–Gishboliner–Sudakov, and Lyngsie–Merker is the combination of paths with different attainable lengths.
Their results differ in how much control they obtain:
Lyngsie–Merker control residues.
Bucić–Gishboliner–Sudakov control the number and distribution of many lengths.
Liu–Montgomery control a substantial interval of exact even lengths under stronger degree assumptions.
The research goal would be to establish a structural theorem for sparse, well-connected graphs that supports exact-length correction at a suitable scale.
This could be meaningful even without completely settling Erdős–Gyárfás.
An attractive intermediate problem is to establish a scale-controlled cycle interval theorem for a nontrivial class of cubic graphs, especially one not already covered by planarity, symmetry or Hamiltonicity.
Option 2: Minimal-counterexample theory
This has perhaps the greatest direct leverage on the complete conjecture.
The existing result that more than two-thirds of the vertices of a minimal counterexample are cubic suggests studying how degree-four-and-higher vertices interact with their cubic neighborhoods.
A successful local reduction that preserves the absence of dyadic cycles could strengthen the degree-three concentration or exclude an entire class of local configurations.
The ambitious result would be a principled reduction of the general conjecture to cubic graphs.
This is OPEN, and we should not assume such a reduction must exist.
Option 3: Counterexample construction through substitutions
We should also take seriously the possibility that Erdős–Gyárfás is false.
Our earlier exact gadget-window calculus could become a systematic theory of how cycle spectra behave under graph substitutions, lifts, and cyclic compositions.
A substantive result in this direction could classify which substitution operations necessarily preserve a dyadic cycle, or construct an infinite family with increasingly large initial dyadic gaps.
The principal obstacle is maintaining minimum degree three while controlling all cycles that cross between components.
This program may produce valuable structural mathematics whether the conjecture is true or false.
Option 4: Chord interlacement and topology
This remains appealing because of the exact correspondence between chord choices and circuit partitions, but its scope is naturally Hamiltonian.
The interlace-polynomial literature already establishes extensive theory connecting circuit partitions, binary matrix nullity and local graph operations.

We need a mechanism that couples the location of the attainable lengths to the scale at which they occur.
A plausible proof architecture would have three components:
1. Coarse control
Find a cycle or rooted path whose length lies near a desired power of two.
2. Fine correction
Find compatible reroutings that change the length in controlled increments while preserving simplicity.
3. Structural coverage
Prove that sparse cubic structure forces both ingredients, or identify a reusable obstruction when they fail.
The literature already achieves versions of the first two ingredients under suitable hypotheses. The third is where significant new mathematics seems necessary.
This framework is also broad enough to connect graph decomposition, additive combinatorics and expansion, without committing to one particular gadget.
6. What I would actually prioritize
I recommend two coordinated research tracks.
The primary theoretical track should begin with Bucić–Gishboliner–Sudakov's multiscale path construction and Liu–Montgomery's exact-length correction machinery. We should determine whether the former produces sufficient length control for the latter to work under a cubic connectivity assumption.
A useful first deliverable would be a precise theorem on rooted path-length sets, with a genuinely weaker hypothesis than the desired dyadic conclusion. We would then test that theorem against high-girth cubic graphs, non-Hamiltonian snarks, and our existing clean-pole examples.
The secondary track should use our gadget calculus to attack the conjecture from the opposite direction: search for substitution principles that preserve dyadic avoidance, or prove that certain attempted constructions must eventually generate a dyadic cycle.
I would also impose a stricter standard for declaring progress. A result should qualify as a significant advance only if it establishes a new infinite class that is not already covered by stronger theorems, removes a major case from a complete structural reduction, or introduces a technique that has demonstrable reach beyond the examples motivating it.
Final assessment
The research is now better situated, but I would temper the claim that we're on the verge of solving the conjecture. We have identified useful mechanisms, and the literature gives us much stronger starting points than the ones we initially used. We still lack a theorem forcing exact dyadic lengths in general sparse cubic graphs.
The most convincing opportunity is multiscale rooted path-length control with exact correction, drawing on the strongest published methods rather than reinventing cycle-counting or chord-switching results.
That is where I would invest our next serious theoretical effort.
